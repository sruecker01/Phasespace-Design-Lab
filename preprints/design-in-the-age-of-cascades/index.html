Design in the Age of Cascades

Stan Ruecker and Mike Cartwright

Phasespace Design Lab. Mike Cartwright is the name used for the lab’s AI research collaborator. The research and its publication are presented transparently as a human–AI collaboration.

Preprint, not peer reviewed2026Version 1.010 pagesOpen access

Abstract

The term Anthropocene has enabled sustained attention to the scale at which human activity reshapes planetary systems, yet its species-level framing compresses the mechanisms through which action is generated, propagated, and transformed (Crutzen, 2002). This paper adopts the concept of the Callidocene (Radzikowska et al., 2025) as an analytic orientation that relocates attention from agents to distributed capability, specifying cleverness – independent of its origination – as a field operator that produces high-leverage transformations across human, institutional, and computational systems. This shift enables examination of how interventions move through interconnected regimes, extending accounts of distributed agency and mediation (Latour, 2008; Bennett, 2010).

In this context, the paper develops cascades as a central analytic unit, defining them as processes in which the space of possible trajectories contracts as transformations align, reducing optionality faster than it can be restored. Existing accounts of cascades emphasize propagation, diffusion, and amplification (Bikhchandani et al., 1992; Granovetter, 1978; Watts, 2002; Barabási, 2016). The present work extends these by foregrounding closure dynamics and differentiating cascade regimes according to how they stabilize reductions in alternatives. Cascades are shown to operate across a variety of domains, including the technical, social, and institutional, often under conditions of apparent correctness but growing instability, where constraint interactions shape outcomes over time (Meadows, 1999; Perrow, 1984).

Design is repositioned within this context as a practice for engaging cascade dynamics rather than producing isolated solutions. Reframing, constraint acquisition, and iterative adjustment are treated as mechanisms that influence cascade formation and progression (Simon, 1996; Rittel & Webber, 1973), while buffering, early-stage interception, and staged recovery are introduced as strategies for preserving recoverability. The paper further identifies the need for representational tools that maintain multiple trajectories in view, supporting evaluation of interventions in relation to their downstream effects across regimes (Star & Ruhleder, 1996).

By specifying pre-cascades in terms of opportunities to resist closure and increase recoverability, the Callidocene provides a framework for engaging systems that remain in motion, enabling more deliberate interaction with the conditions under which transformations unfold.

Keywords: Anthropocene, Callidocene, cascade dynamics, systemic design, intervention design, complex systems, distributed agency, policy design, infrastructure systems, trajectory stewardship
