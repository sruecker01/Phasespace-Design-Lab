Can We Grow Dandelions the Size of Rhubarb as a Food Crop?

25 November 2026 · Version 1.1 · Research in Progress

Download the research letter (PDF)Supporting calculations (ZIP)

In Brief

Imagine a dandelion the size of a rhubarb plant, with abundant edible leaves and a substantial, inulin-rich root. Could genetic engineering turn this familiar plant into a productive food crop? Our investigation has taken us into comparative genomics, root development and carbohydrate storage. We have analyzed archived sequence data, executed root-growth models and identified a more detailed simulator to investigate. The results expose resource tradeoffs and a gap in our understanding of development. We have not produced a giant dandelion, demonstrated an improved food crop, or run the new simulator. This letter describes our progress toward that possibility.

From a little rosette to a food crop

Russian dandelion, Taraxacum koksaghyz, stores inulin, a carbohydrate, and natural rubber in its roots. Wieghaus and colleagues used CRISPR/Cas9 to disrupt TkRALFL1 and observed altered root form, greater biomass and increased inulin yield in greenhouse plants. Those authors’ results establish a precedent for this species; they do not demonstrate our proposed plant or an improved common dandelion. [1]

Rhubarb gives us an image of the scale of the whole plant, not a specified root dimension. We want a larger harvest of usable leaves and root, not merely a thicker root or a higher inulin concentration. The wild type is our experimental control, not our design ceiling. Our present calculations concern roots; the leaves, their quality and the health of the whole plant remain to be investigated.

Could we change the way a dandelion grows?

The published root transcriptome study compared Russian dandelions with different root forms and identified genes whose activity was associated with those differences. A glucanase called TkBG11, identified as GWHPAAAA042727, showed greater expression in thinner roots. The authors also tested that association in a separate set of plants. This is evidence of an association; it does not establish that suppressing the gene would enlarge a root. [2]

Our completed reanalysis examined another glucanase-family candidate, GWHPAAAA003778. That distinction matters: related genes cannot inherit each other’s experimental support merely because they share a family name. We processed 14,498,640 archived reads from three root-tip pools and used sequence markers designed to distinguish the candidate from other coding sequences. We then compared a shared sequencing flowcell and the same first 75 bases of eligible reads, reducing two technical differences between the files. [3]

The candidate remains less abundant in the thick-root pool after those controls. An independent implementation reproduced the assignments in all 798 diagnostic reads retained across the screened genes. This supports the counting procedure and the direction of the comparison in the retrieved files. [3]

Why the qualification belongs beside the result

The candidate counts are sparse, and each root form is represented by one pooled library. These files do not provide biological replication for estimating the reliability of the difference. The retrieved European Nucleotide Archive records are marked SUPPRESSED, without an explanation in those records. Their totals agree with the files we processed, but differ from the cleaned totals reported in the paper. The exact correspondence remains unresolved. [3]

For the larger food-crop ambition, this is a qualified lead rather than an established growth mechanism. We cannot infer a causal edit effect, choose an intervention strength, or convert the abundance ratios into predicted root growth. Resolving archive provenance and measuring the candidate in independent plants are necessary before promoting it beyond that status.

The same harvest can hide different histories

Suppose two plants contain the same amount of inulin at harvest. One might have directed a large share of incoming material into storage throughout development. Another might have accumulated less initially but retained more of it later. A final measurement alone cannot tell these histories apart. This is the identification problem we investigated with a simple model that separates inulin from other root material. [3]

We replayed the original model and extended it to include a finite storage capacity, a competing retained pool and explicit losses. The extension asks how a root might divide an effective incoming supply among storage and other uses. In a selected grid, 49 of 81 parameter cases matched the same final root mass and inulin fraction within the specified tolerance, despite differing intermediate trajectories. The grid is a set of computational comparisons, not a probability distribution over real plants. [3]

Keep the amount and its denominator together

For an individual root measured consistently, total inulin is its dry mass multiplied by its inulin fraction. A concentration can rise while the amount stays constant if the remaining root material decreases. Root volume adds another distinction: a change in shape or water content need not represent a proportional change in dry matter. These quantities answer different questions.

Inulin per root = root dry mass × inulin fraction of dry mass

This apparently simple accounting also changed our calibration. The 2019 paper reports a control mean dry mass of 1.89 g and an inulin fraction of 0.499, whose product is approximately 0.943 g. Its separately reported mean inulin yield is 1.08 g. The measurements involve different sampled cohorts; multiplying separate group means does not reconstruct a directly measured average plant. We used the product as a representative modeling target and retained the reported yield as a separate benchmark. [1, 3]

The model’s incoming supply is expressed in dry-matter-equivalent units. Upstream conversion into that supply lies outside its boundary. It does not simulate a complete leaf canopy, photosynthesis, water acquisition or reproduction. Numerical conservation checks show that material is accounted for within the defined boundary; they do not establish that a real plant can provide the assumed supply.

For a rhubarb-scale plant, we would need to follow root mass, absolute inulin and its concentration at several developmental stages, alongside leaf and shoot growth. Intermediate observations can distinguish mechanisms that a successful-looking harvest endpoint leaves unresolved. The eventual food-crop model must account for both edible organs.

An improvement can move the constraint

We next tested hypothetical changes in plant function under the same total incoming resource budget. The chosen changes were 25% more storage capacity per unit of other root material, a 10% relative increase in requested allocation to inulin, and a 25% reduction in the rate parameter governing inulin turnover. Each began at the start of the modeled period. These magnitudes are exploratory assumptions; no gene, edit or symbiont has been shown to produce them in our plant. [3]

All values are model outputs relative to the same baseline and input budget. “Other root material” excludes inulin and the separate competing retained pool. It is not a direct measurement of structural strength or plant health. [3]

The combined case illustrates the issue. Inulin rises by about 18%, but total root mass increases by less than 2%, while other root material falls by about 16%. That is an interesting redistribution to investigate. Its value would depend on what was reduced, whether the plant remained viable, and whether the assumed functional changes were achievable. The model cannot answer those biological questions.

Testing the factors separately and together also prevents an easy mistake: multiplying attractive individual effects as though they were independent. In this model, allocation, capacity and turnover interact through the same changing pools. Their combination must be computed under the same resource assumptions and compared with an explicit baseline.

What the successful checks establish

The integrated analysis passed 17 numerical checks. Independent comparisons recovered the earlier model in the appropriate limiting case, checked conservation and tested sensitivity to numerical step size. Those checks strengthen confidence that the equations were executed as intended. They do not validate the equations as a description of dandelion biology. [3]

For our food-crop ambition, we need to discover which changes could increase usable root and leaf output while supporting the plant’s other functions, and what observations would show that a proposed mechanism is wrong.

A failed developmental fit is a finding

We challenged the model with approximate control observations digitized from a developmental figure in Benninghaus and colleagues’ study. Nine simple supply-history and turnover combinations were fitted to inulin amount and concentration at eight and twelve weeks. All nine fitted the early observations poorly. The best produced approximately 0.187 g of inulin at twelve weeks against 1.35 g in the figure. At the unfitted twenty-four-week point, it produced 0.748 g against approximately 2.725 g. [3, 4]

Perturbing the graph readings within selected extraction bounds did not remove the discrepancy. The study used pooled plants at the earliest age and individuals later, limiting precision. These tested profiles inadequately describe the dataset; they do not establish a biological ceiling. Early calibration already failed. The calculations completed, and the mismatch is a substantive result. [3]

A broader model, still to be assessed

MeOSR is a promising candidate because it connects three-dimensional cassava root development with carbon allocation and storage-root thickening. Its authors compared early growth with field observations spanning 28–84 days. That work concerns cassava cultivar KU50 and starch storage; it does not validate dandelion growth or inulin metabolism. Its case-specific water and nutrient behavior was not validated, and final crop yield lay beyond the tested period. [5]

We acquired the authors’ source code and completed documentary registration in the shared simulator catalogue. We have not compiled or run MeOSR, reproduced its publication, or calibrated it for dandelion. The requested supporting dataset payload was not acquired. The publication’s exact code revision also remains unconfirmed. Registration records a resource for reuse; it does not establish reproduction or biological applicability. [6, 7]

What would a rhubarb-scale dandelion require?

The next step is to resolve missing author materials and source-version provenance, then attempt a bounded build and baseline reproduction before deciding about adaptation. A dandelion successor should be tested across development against independent observations. Baseline, individual changes and combinations should be compared under matched resource conditions, with extra resources explicitly accounted for.

A rhubarb-scale food crop would need more than extra root storage. We would measure plant spread, leaf and root fresh and dry mass, absolute inulin, tissue composition and reproduction across development. Taste, tenderness, food safety and ecological effects require separate assessment. The ambition is to increase the amount of usable food the plant can produce while remaining healthy and viable, not simply to improve one constituent of the root.

How to read the evidence

Published plant experiments are attributed to their authors. Our archive reanalysis describes the retrieved pools, with suppression status, sparse counts and provenance limitations retained. Functional percentages are conditional outputs of executed equations, with no established gene-to-parameter mapping. Developmental graph readings are approximate; extraction tolerances are not biological confidence intervals. Software checks establish defined computational behavior, not whole-plant validity.

This interim letter adds no new biological experiment. Its preparation verified preserved outputs and arithmetic without rerunning the underlying study or MeOSR. The project has not demonstrated edited plants, field performance, reproductive effects, food suitability or ecological outcomes. The proposed build, reproduction and adaptation work remains to be performed.
